# Data Management Plan

# Section 1: Project Overview
 Our project will become an open-access digital archive to show how space photography has evolved since the 1950s. Our collection directly contrasts grainy, historical glass film plates with high-definition, infrared imagery from modern telescopes like Hubble and James Webb. By sourcing public domain and Creative Commons assets from NASA, JPL, and NOIRLab, we are tracking the massive leap in visual clarity achieved once technology moved past Earth's atmospheric limitations. 
Technically, this archive is built as a structured metadata project using CollectionBuilder-Sheets to convert historical imagery into clean, machine-readable data. Every asset is tracked using normalized fields like unique object IDs, exact capture dates, and specific telescope or observatory origins, ensuring the collection functions as an empirical database rather than a static photo album. Ultimately, this open-access structure makes the collection a versatile, real-world educational tool, allowing students, teachers, and independently curious astronomers to easily integrate the data into interactive timelines, study digital asset management, or analyze the technical evolution of how we map the universe.

# Section 3: Data Inventory & Provenance
## File inventory
   We are including mostly JPGs, PNGs, and Webp files to easily access the photos on the website.
## Tools and access
Our project is an analysis of data management frameworks to build a professional-grade digital repository to show the history of Space Imaging. The technical foundation of our archive relies on a cloud-based workflow where a highly structured Google Sheet serves as our primary metadata inventory. Cataloging every single item with standardized fields for critical information while integrating a master data spreadsheet into a GitHub repository using the CollectionBuilder framework will create a searchable website with an extensive gallery that is also easy to grow and maintain. Because this is a public digital humanities project, we are also focusing on digital ethics and intellectual property. We are strictly using public domain assets from government entities like JPL and NASA, and being mindful of other possible factors like Creative Commons licenses for the European Space Agency (ESA) and ESO.

# Section 5: Access, Reuse & Rights
## Your license
The license we have chosen for our created content is the Creative Commons license. This will allow others to share, use or build upon our work.
## Rights implications of your sources
All of the items used in our collection are in the public domain; those rights will also dictate what others are allowed to do with items in our collection.
## Ethical considerations
In our collection we have two images of individuals Buzz Aldrin and Bruce McCandless that have the potential for ethical considerations. Most of the images in our collection are of celestial bodies with photos taken from spacecraft which avoids ethical issues. 

## Appendix: Data Dictionary
For our file-naming convention, we aimed to keep it simple. We included keywords for what was in the photo being used, what instrument was used to take the photo, and any other standout details. We also included an ‘S’ or ‘G’ in the file name to indicate whether the photo was taken in space or from Earth. Most of our files had unique convenient titles; we didn’t need to come up with our own unique ones. As we were choosing photographs of celestial bodies, it was clear what we would need to include in our subjects section.
